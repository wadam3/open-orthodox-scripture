# Why Swete? Historical origin, strengths, limitations, and working-source rationale

**Status:** Research and editorial discussion, not a declaration that an English translation is accurate, reviewed, or approved by any Orthodox Church. Last reviewed: 2026-11-12.

## What this document is for

This is the project record of **why Henry Barclay Swete's historical Greek Septuagint edition is our provisional starting point**, how it came to exist, and what we still need to investigate. It concerns **Swete's Greek source text**, not a comparison or adaptation of modern English Bible translations.

Our goal is to produce an **original** English translation from documented, independently usable Greek material, with careful attention to Eastern Orthodox and particularly Greek Orthodox usage. An editor's denomination does not determine the religious orientation of the ancient manuscript readings; nevertheless, important decisions about which readings to follow must be documented and later reviewed by qualified Orthodox readers.

## Two different historical origins

**The Septuagint:** Ancient Greek translations of Jewish Scriptures began with the Torah, probably in third-century BCE Alexandria. Other books were translated or composed in Greek over the ensuing centuries. There was no single surviving original manuscript containing the entire collection. The texts were transmitted through many manuscript traditions and subsequently became foundational to Greek-speaking Christian worship and biblical interpretation.

**Swete's edition:** Henry Barclay Swete (1835–1917), a Cambridge-based Anglican scholar, did **not** translate the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek. He edited and published an existing ancient **Greek** textual tradition from surviving manuscript witnesses. His editorial decisions and the source manuscripts must be distinguished from the ancient writings themselves.

## How Swete's edition came about

- **1883:** The Cambridge University Press Septuagint project was entrusted to Swete as a convenient, scholarly Greek text based on a principal ancient manuscript with significant alternative readings noted.
- **1887:** Volume I, **Genesis–4 Kingdoms**, appeared.
- **1891:** Volume II, **1 Chronicles–Tobit**, appeared.
- **1894:** Volume III, **Hosea–4 Maccabees**, completed the three-volume first edition.
- Later revised printings exist. **For the Genesis pilot, the working reference is explicitly Volume I, first edition, 1887**, rather than a later revision silently substituted for it.

Swete's main running-text witness was the **fourth-century Codex Vaticanus** (often identified as B), supplemented where incomplete by other early Greek witnesses, especially the **fifth-century Codex Alexandrinus** (A). His notes record selected textual variants. This is a historical scholarly edition, not an unmediated copy of a single ancient Bible and not a new Greek translation.

### Important qualification for Genesis

The original surviving portions of Codex Vaticanus **lack Genesis 1:1–46:28**. So the opening of Genesis in Swete **cannot** come from the surviving original fourth-century Vaticanus leaves; Swete used supplementary witnesses there, principally the Alexandrinus tradition. The exact adopted readings and manuscript markers for **Genesis 1** still need to be checked directly against the **1887 first edition**. Treating every verse of Swete as simply "Vaticanus" would be inaccurate.

For the Maccabean books, where Vaticanus does not provide the necessary text, Swete's own later preface identifies **Codex Alexandrinus** as the primary source for the running text. Thus, **different books can have different manuscript bases** within his edition.

## Why we chose it as a *provisional* base

1. **Traceable history:** A definite editor, edition, printing date, and manuscript-based editorial method make it possible to explain what the Greek text is and how it was assembled.
2. **Independently reusable historical source:** The **1887 printed edition** is in the public domain in the United States by publication date. We can prepare an original translation without requesting source-text permissions. This conclusion does **not** automatically extend to third-party modern transcriptions, apparatus, annotations, or service terms.
3. **Septuagint tradition:** The Greek Old Testament has a deep place in Eastern Orthodox, especially Greek Orthodox, worship and interpretation. Beginning with Greek sources follows that tradition more directly than starting by adapting a modern English translation.
4. **Broad coverage:** The full three-volume edition gives us a substantial starting corpus, including books absent from many shorter Old Testament collections.
5. **Variants are visible:** Its witness-based method supports the project's plan to record meaningful disagreements instead of silently harmonizing passages.

## Limitations and cautions

- **Not officially Orthodox:** Swete was a scholar, not an ecclesiastical authority for the Greek Orthodox Church. His edition is **not** an officially approved Greek Orthodox Bible.
- **Not one untouched original:** It combines a preferred manuscript with supplements and editorial choices, and the manuscript basis differs by book.
- **Older scholarship:** Subsequent manuscript discoveries and textual research can expose readings or distinctions not adequately covered by an edition of the 1880s–1890s.
- **Historical witness is not always identical to liturgical usage:** When independently demonstrable Greek Orthodox textual practice differs materially from our working base, we must investigate rather than automatically favor either version.
- **Book organization is not identical to our repository:** The order, divisions, alternative Greek forms, and additions require a documented mapping to our planned Orthodox Old Testament workspaces.
- **Digitization is a separate rights question:** An ancient text and a public-domain historical printed edition are not permission to copy arbitrary modern digitizations, critical notes, or English translations.
- **Geographic rights:** United States public-domain status does not automatically settle the law of every country where a future edition may be distributed.

## How we will keep the project Orthodox-oriented without borrowing existing translations

We will translate the Greek itself and preserve what the selected textual witness actually says. We will **not** copy or lightly rewrite existing English Bibles or commentaries, whether Orthodox, Protestant, or otherwise. Nor will we silently change Greek words to make a passage match a preferred doctrine.

When a meaningful question arises, the book-specific log must separately record:

1. **Textual evidence:** What the Greek source says, which verifiable ancient witnesses differ, and the exact chapter/verse references.
2. **Translation choice:** How that evidence was expressed in original English, with alternatives and uncertainty.
3. **Orthodox-use question:** What aspect of Orthodox liturgical or interpretive usage needs further consideration, without claiming endorsement or inventing an authoritative reading.
4. **Reader explanation:** A concise original footnote/explainer tied to the passage, based on independently usable evidence.

The **initial full-Bible draft remains provisional**. Qualified external Greek-language and Orthodox theological review occurs **after the complete draft**, consistent with the project's existing protocol. Separating source evidence from interpretation is essential: a meaningful theological question does not by itself establish a manuscript discrepancy.

## Flagged textual and source-history differences — investigation queue (2026-11-12)

**Status: OPEN / not yet verified passage by passage.** The following are **priority checks**, not assertions that Swete altered Scripture or that a particular disputed Greek wording has already been resolved. Do not convert any flag directly into a reader-facing footnote until the precise historical Greek evidence is independently checked. Preserve source-specific evidence and decisions in the relevant book's separate `notes/ot/` workspace; add explanatory footnotes when a *substantive, verified* textual difference is identified under [the translation protocol](TRANSLATION_PROTOCOL.md).

- **SWETE-FLAG-001 — Vaticanus-preferred editorial text versus other Greek witnesses.** Swete typically used Codex Vaticanus as the running-text base where available, with supplements and selected variant readings. Ancient manuscripts do not always agree. **Check:** For each substantive variance encountered, identify Swete's exact printed reading and manuscript basis, locate independently verifiable alternative ancient Greek evidence, distinguish a genuinely different reading from a difference in spelling/word division, and record whether the English meaning changes. **Status:** methodology documented; individual passages pending research.
- **SWETE-FLAG-002 — Genesis 1 is not preserved in the surviving original Vaticanus leaves.** Vaticanus lacks the beginning of Genesis (through 46:28 in the surviving manuscript), so the Greek of Genesis 1 in Swete comes from supplementary evidence, particularly Alexandrinus. **Check:** Inspect the **1887 Volume I first-edition pages and printed witness markers**; identify the actual text used for Genesis 1:1–31 and log the exact page-to-verse mapping before transcribing. A missing manuscript section is **a source-provenance issue, not automatically a wording discrepancy or translation error**. **Status:** high-priority prerequisite to the first chapter.
- **SWETE-FLAG-003 — Two major Greek forms of Daniel.** The older Greek form of Daniel and the Greek version traditionally associated with Theodotion differ significantly. The Theodotion-associated form predominated in historic Greek Christian and Orthodox usage; Swete discussed the relationship and printed both forms. **Check:** Verify the exact presentation of both forms in the relevant historical Swete volume, and establish an explicit Orthodox-oriented editorial rule for the primary Daniel text and treatment of the alternative. No version may be silently conflated with the other. **Status:** book-level choice unresolved.
- **SWETE-FLAG-004 — Daniel 7:13 and Greek additions as candidate passage checks.** Daniel 7:13 is one example where the Greek Daniel traditions warrant direct comparison. The Prayer of Azariah/Song of the Three, Susanna, and Bel and the Dragon also need careful source-form and location mapping. **Check:** Verify the relevant Greek wording, sequence, and verse references independently; note any substantive differences and Orthodox-use implications in `notes/ot/DAN/`, then link appropriate original explanatory footnotes. **Status:** examples queued, not reviewed or resolved.

**Evidence and publishing rule:** Identify historical manuscripts and public-domain editions accurately in the internal research log; do **not** copy protected modern apparatus or electronic transcriptions, and do not solicit licenses. Ordinary reader-facing notes should describe the actual verified difference in clear, generic language without unnecessary proprietary names. Distinguish *scholarly disagreement about a reading* from an allegation of intentional alteration by the editor. The existence of a flag does not constitute a confirmed error, approved footnote, or Church endorsement.

**Historical background for the Daniel flags:** Swete himself discusses the older Greek and Theodotion-associated versions in [his *Introduction to the Old Testament in Greek*, additional notes](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/swete/greekot.iii.ii.html); his discussion of Greek additions is in [the same work](https://ccel.org/ccel/swete/greekot/greekot.iv.ii.html). These historical references guide verification; they do not substitute for checking the selected printed edition.

## Licensing and evidence safeguards

- The project will **not request or negotiate permissions** to reuse a Greek source edition. If a required source component cannot be used on independently verified public-domain terms, we choose another route.
- We may accurately identify **historical scholars, manuscripts, book titles, and publication facts** in internal scholarly documentation. Identification is not endorsement or dependence on a proprietary product.
- No restricted modern English translation, digital Greek compilation, or proprietary critical apparatus is to be imported or paraphrased as a substitute for independent textual research.
- The project may eventually apply an openly reusable license to **its own original translation and original notes**; that is distinct from relying on a third-party source license.
